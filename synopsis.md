# Synopsis

## Logline

A mortal girl becomes the monster the gods need her to be, and learns too late that the only story left to her is the one told by the boy sent to kill her.

## One-paragraph pitch

From a cave at the edge of the known world, Medusa tells the story that will otherwise belong to Perseus. She was born mortal among immortal, already monstrous sisters, loved by them because she could be lost; when a travelling priest noticed her at twelve, she spent two years imagining Athena's temple until, at fourteen, she chose Athens. There, devotion makes her visible to the wrong god. After Poseidon violates the sanctuary and Athena answers by cursing the girl rather than the god, Medusa's beauty becomes a weapon: her hair lives, her gaze turns mortal men to stone, and her sisters take her west before the world can finish naming her. Years later, surrounded by the statues of heroes who came to win glory from her body, Medusa hears winged sandals above the shore. Perseus arrives with Athena's shield. Medusa recognises both the cruelty of the old story and the shape of a final choice inside it.

## Full synopsis

Medusa speaks from the cave where she lives with her sisters Stheno and Euryale, in the days before Perseus arrives. She knows someone is coming. The knowledge has no single source: the sea sounds different, her serpents wake before she does, and her sisters move through their small domestic rituals with a care that feels like mourning. Around the cave stand the men who came for her before him, each caught forever at the moment he understood what he had sought.

She begins with Sarpedon. Unlike Stheno and Euryale, Medusa is mortal. Her sisters are already old when she is born, already monstrous in ways the world has taught them to survive, and they love her with the urgency of creatures who cannot die and therefore know too well what it means that she can. As a child, Medusa learns their forms before she learns to fear them: wings, bronze hands, serpents, song. Her first memories are of storms, salt, and the sisters lifting her out of danger.

At twelve, a travelling priest notices her beauty and stillness. He proposes that she be sent to Athens to serve Athena. Stheno understands the honour and the danger. Euryale goes silent. Medusa wants to go. For two years she imagines the goddess as an answer to every shapeless longing in her: order, attention, purpose, a love large enough to sanctify her life. At fourteen she leaves Sarpedon by choice, believing that the choice makes her free.

Athens is smaller and harsher than she imagined, but the temple becomes home. Medusa learns the rites, the handling of lamps and offerings, the language of clean floors and folded cloth. She loves the wooden image of Athena with a devotion so complete that it feels returned whenever light shifts across the goddess's face. In the temple she finds discipline, friendship, and the dangerous joy of being favoured. She also begins to feel watched. Because she is young, and because she wants the gaze to be holy, she mistakes it for Athena.

Poseidon comes on a night when the lamps are low. The scene is remembered in fragments: brine where no sea should be, the old olive tree outside, flame leaning as if in wind, the floor hard beneath her hands. Medusa does not make a spectacle of her own suffering. She tells the fact plainly because the world will otherwise dress it in better language for the god.

At dawn, Athena appears. Her altar has been defiled. Her temple has been made the site of another god's power, and she cannot or will not punish Poseidon. She turns instead to the girl who loved her. Athena's curse makes Medusa's body bear the argument between gods: serpents for hair, a face no mortal man may survive, beauty transformed into punishment and armour at once. The first death is small and unbearable, a temple dog caught by accident in Medusa's gaze. Medusa touches the stone ear and understands what has been made of her.

Stheno and Euryale come for her without asking what happened. They carry her west, blindfolded, away from Athens and away from any mortal place that might make a story of her too soon. On the journey, a shepherd sees her by accident and dies. In the far west, beyond the ordinary maps, the sisters find the cave. Euryale says they have been there before, or that places like it have always been waiting for them.

Years pass. Medusa learns the rules of her curse by living inside them. Men turn to stone. Women do not. Gods cannot be touched by it. Animals can, so she stops looking at birds. The reflected gaze is dangerous in a different way. Her serpents become part of her, hungry and alert, sometimes more honest than her mouth. Stheno hunts, mends, cooks, and arranges the statues. Euryale sings the old songs until fear silences her.

The heroes begin to arrive. Some come for glory, some for reward, some because a father, king, or beloved has made Medusa's head the price of their manhood. She tells herself she pities them. Sometimes she does. Sometimes she keeps them because their stone faces are the only faces that can look at her now. A girl, the daughter of the dead shepherd, wanders into the cave and does not turn to stone. Medusa lets her go. The encounter teaches her that the curse is not as simple as Athena's sentence, and that monstrosity can be a door as well as a prison.

By the time Perseus comes, Medusa is no longer only the girl from Sarpedon or the priestess from Athens. She is the narrator of a life that has been taken from her piece by piece, and she has arranged what remains. Perseus arrives young, frightened, and obedient, carrying a polished shield from Athena and a blade meant to make a legend of him. Medusa sees the goddess's instruction in the way he holds the bronze. He does not look at her directly. In the reflection, she sees a boy sent by powers older and colder than he is.

Medusa understands that he is not mercy, not justice, and not forgiveness. He is an ending. The gods have left her very little, but inside the shape of this ending there remains one act that can belong to her. She does not rush him. She does not make him kill a flinching thing. With Stheno's rage and Euryale's grief behind her, with Athena's shield before her, Medusa closes her eyes.

The blade itself stays off the page. The story ends before Perseus can make it his.

## What this story is about

Divine injustice. The grammar of blame. The way a woman's body becomes a site of argument between men and gods. Sisterhood as the only reliable love. Monstrosity as armour, and the cost of wearing armour long enough to forget the shape of yourself underneath. Agency at the edge of fate.

## What this story is not

A revenge fantasy. A romance. A redemption of Poseidon or Athena. A rehabilitation of Perseus. Medusa does not win. She chooses.
